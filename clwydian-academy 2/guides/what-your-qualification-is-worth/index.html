Regulated credit or a certificate? What your qualification is actually worth

Two courses can teach the same technique, on the same day, to the same standard in the room — and leave you holding two completely different things. This explains the difference, why the prices are so far apart, and when the cheaper one is the right buy.

Regulated means something specific

A regulated qualification sits on a national framework. An awarding body writes the standard, an assessor judges your work against it, an internal quality assurer checks the assessor, and the awarding body’s own external verification checks the centre. The credit is recorded against you and it counts toward a full qualification.

An unregulated certificate is issued by whoever ran the course. It may be excellent training. It may be exactly what your insurer wants to see. But the standard it was judged against is the provider’s own, and the certificate proves attendance and competence to that provider’s satisfaction, not to a national one.

Both are legitimate. They are priced differently because they are different products, and the trouble starts when they are compared on price alone.

What the price gap is actually made of

Guided learning hours. A regulated unit carries a stated number — the awarding body’s figure for the teaching, practice and supervised assessment the unit requires. Our CPD units run from 8 hours to 158. A two-day attendance course is twelve to fourteen hours of contact. When one product costs three times another and contains ten times the hours, the price per hour is going the other way.

Assessment and quality assurance. Someone has to observe you on real clients, build the evidence, quality assure it internally, and submit it. Some units add an external written paper. That cost falls on a one-day award as heavily as on a ten-week unit, which is why our published price formula has a fixed component as well as an hourly one.

Registration. The awarding body charges to register a learner against a unit. No registration, no regulated credit.

Portability. This is the part that matters years later. Regulated credit follows you. If you move salons, move countries, or come back to finish a Diploma in five years, the units you banked are still yours.

When the cheaper course is the right purchase

Often. If you are a qualified stylist who wants to offer a specific branded system next month, and your insurer accepts the certificate, a short course is the correct buy and a ten-week regulated unit is over-engineering. Nobody needs regulated credit in a technique they will use twice.

Buy the regulated unit when you are building toward a qualification, when you want the credit to count for something later, when the service carries real risk and you want an external standard behind your competence — or when your employer, insurer or awarding body is going to ask what standard you were assessed against.

How to tell which you are being sold

Ask four questions. A regulated course answers all four in a sentence. If the answers are vague, you are being sold an attendance certificate, which is fine — as long as that is what you meant to buy.

Who is the awarding body?

A regulated course names it — VTCT, for every unit on our calendar. “Accredited” on its own is not an answer; it means a provider has been approved by somebody, not that your credit is recorded on a national framework.

What is the unit code?

Every regulated unit has one — UHB205, UNHB374 — and it identifies the exact standard you are assessed against. An attendance course has a course name and no code, because there is no national standard behind it.

How many guided learning hours?

A regulated unit carries the awarding body’s figure for the teaching, practice and supervised assessment it requires; ours run from 8 to 158. If the answer is “two days” rather than a number of guided hours, you are looking at contact time on an attendance course.

Does the credit count toward a full qualification, and which one?

Regulated credit is recorded against you and banks toward a Diploma — it follows you if you move salons or come back to finish in five years. A certificate proves attendance and competence to the provider’s satisfaction and counts toward nothing else. Either can be the right buy, as long as it is the one you meant to buy.
