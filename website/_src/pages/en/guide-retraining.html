Retraining as a hairdresser in Wales, as an adult

Most guides to becoming a hairdresser are written for sixteen-year-olds. This one is for people who already have a job, a rent payment and possibly children, and who are working out whether the trade is a realistic thing to move into. It covers what the qualifications actually are, what they cost in Wales, what funding does and does not exist, and how long each route takes.

The first thing to be clear about: the Personal Learning Account will not pay for this

If you have searched for funded training in Wales you will have met the Personal Learning Account. It is a genuine and generous programme — and it does not cover hairdressing or barbering.

Medr, the body that funds tertiary education in Wales, runs the PLA against six national priority sectors: advanced materials and manufacturing, digital, health and social care, hospitality and tourism, net zero within construction, energy, engineering and manufacturing, and transport and logistics. Hair is not among them. There is no exclusion clause to argue with; the sector simply is not on the list.

There is a second gate that catches people out even in a covered sector. A PLA requires you to be nineteen or over, employed or self-employed, and earning under the Welsh median salary. Someone between jobs and looking to retrain — the exact person who most wants it — would not qualify even if hairdressing were listed.

We would rather tell you that on the first page than let you find out after an application. It is also, frankly, a position we argue about publicly: this is a trade that employs thousands of people across Wales and it sits outside the funding architecture built for retraining.

So what does pay for it

An apprenticeship. If you are employed in a salon, the Welsh apprenticeship framework funds the training in full. You earn while you qualify and your employer pays your wages, not your training. This is the cheapest route into the trade by a distance and the one most people should look at first. It requires a salon willing to take you on.

ReAct+. If you have been made redundant, the Welsh Government’s ReAct+ programme can contribute up to £1,500 toward training costs. Eligibility is assessed individually and it is worth a conversation with Working Wales before you assume either way.

Paying for it yourself, over time. Our fast-track NVQ Level 2 is {{fee:nvq}}, payable as {{fee:deposit}} and then {{fee:instalment}} a month for {{fee:instalmentsWord}} months. That is a real amount of money and we are not going to pretend otherwise. What it buys is set out in the guide on what a qualification is worth.

The qualification landscape, in plain terms

There are three things people call “a hairdressing course” and they are not interchangeable.

A short attendance course teaches one technique over a day or two and ends in a certificate an insurer will usually accept. It is a legitimate product — a working stylist adding a service buys one of these. It carries no regulated credit and it does not make anyone qualified.

A single regulated unit is assessed against an awarding body’s standard, externally quality assured, and counts toward a Diploma as one of its units. It proves competence in one area of practice. On its own it does not make you a hairdresser either.

An NVQ Level 2 Diploma is what the trade means when it says qualified. It is the whole occupational standard — cutting, colouring, styling, consultation, health and safety, working under appointment pressure — assessed across every unit, on real clients rather than blocks. It is what an employer, an insurer, or a landlord renting you a chair is asking about.

Level 3 is the senior stylist ladder: colour correction, the technical services that command premium prices, and the units that let you charge differently.

One thing worth understanding, because almost nobody explains it: in the newer VTCT qualification family, some Level 3 units sit inside the Level 2 diploma. In the barbering diploma two of them are mandatory, so every graduate finishes holding Level 3 credit. Take the Level 3 options as well and you leave a Level 2 course with four Level 3 units behind you. That is not a marketing line, it is how the qualification is built, and it changes what you can do next.

How long it actually takes

A traditional college route runs to the academic year: two years, September start, term times.

An apprenticeship in Wales typically runs eighteen months to two years per level, in the salon, with a trainer visiting you.

Our fast-track Level 2 runs {{fee:monthsWord}} months — two academy days a fortnight, with the theory timetabled as distance learning between them so every day in the academy is spent with your hands in hair. Barbering is different: {{fee:barberingWeeks}} weeks, one evening a week on a Tuesday, five until nine, with distance learning carrying the rest. It is built for people who cannot give up daytime work.

The honest comparison is not which course is shortest. It is how many months you spend not yet earning as a qualified stylist.

Is it too late

The question we are asked most often, usually by people in their thirties and forties, and occasionally with an apology attached.
